### Key Finding 1 — GDP and Nikkei Diverge
The correlation between Japan's GDP and Nikkei 225 performance is 
-0.36, showing that over the 2014-2023 decade, the two measures 
tended to move in opposite directions rather than together. This 
divergence is driven by the yen's dual effect: a weaker yen converts 
export revenue earned in dollars into more yen, boosting profits and 
stock prices for export-heavy companies like Toyota that dominate 
the Nikkei, while the same weak yen makes energy imports more 
expensive in yen terms, dragging down GDP. The clearest example is 
2022, when GDP fell sharply from $5.23T in 2021 to $4.45T, while 
Nikkei only dipped slightly from 28,837 to 27,258 — remaining near 
its decade-high level rather than crashing alongside GDP. This 
divergence means an investor watching only the Nikkei would conclude 
Japan's economy is thriving, while an ordinary citizen dealing with 
rising import costs and a shrinking dollar-GDP would experience the 
opposite — the two measures tell fundamentally different stories 
about who benefits and who suffers from a weak yen.

### Key Finding 2 — Inflation and Nikkei Share a Root Cause
The correlation between Japan's inflation rate and Nikkei 225 
performance is +0.24 — a weak positive relationship that runs 
opposite to standard theory, which predicts inflation should scare 
investors and push stocks down. This apparent contradiction resolves 
once the underlying mechanism is examined: both inflation and Nikkei 
rose together in 2021-2023 because they share the same root cause — 
a weakening yen. The weak yen drove up the yen-cost of energy and 
import goods (raising inflation) while simultaneously boosting the 
yen-value of export revenue for Nikkei's dominant companies (raising 
stock prices). Inflation is not directly causing Nikkei to rise; both 
are independent effects of the same currency movement. This distinction 
matters because it's a reminder that correlation between two economic 
indicators can reflect a shared external cause rather than one metric 
driving the other — an assumption worth checking before drawing 
conclusions from any correlation in this analysis.

### Key Finding 3 — Pure Exporters Respond More Strongly to the Yen
USD/JPY correlates at +0.63 with Toyota's stock price but only +0.43 
with Sony's — both positive, but meaningfully different in strength. 
Toyota is a nearly pure export play, so a weak yen converts its 
dollar-denominated overseas revenue directly into higher yen profits. 
Sony's business is more diversified — PlayStation hardware, gaming, 
image sensors, music, and film — meaning some revenue streams benefit 
from a weak yen while others (like international production costs) 
are exposed to the opposite effect, diluting the relationship. This 
mirrors a pattern already identified in the Japan Trade Intelligence 
project: Automotive ran a +$132bn net export surplus while Electronics 
actually ran a -$13bn net deficit, meaning Japan is structurally a 
net IMPORTER of electronics today. The same structural weakness that 
showed up in trade flow data appears again here in individual stock 
behavior — a weaker export position for a sector translates directly 
into a weaker yen-sensitivity for that sector's flagship company.

### Key Finding 4 — BOJ Rate Alone Is a Weak Predictor
The correlation between Japan's BOJ interest rate and USD/JPY is -0.22, confirming the expected direction — lower rates associated with a weaker yen — but the relationship is notably weak compared to other correlations in this analysis.
This weakness makes sense once currency mechanics are considered: exchange rates are driven by the RELATIVE difference between two countries' interest rates, not by either country's rate in isolation — if both the BOJ and the US Federal Reserve have low rates simultaneously, the yen may stay stable even with near-zero Japanese rates.
In 2022 specifically, the BOJ kept its rate near zero while the US Fed raised rates aggressively to fight inflation — it was this widening gap between the two central banks, not BOJ's rate alone, that drove USD/JPY to ¥131.
This finding highlights a genuine limitation of this analysis: without US Fed rate data to calculate the actual rate differential, this correlation only captures half the mechanism — a more complete v2 analysis would pull Fed rate data and test the gap directly.

### Key Finding 5 — GDP/Yen Mechanical Link
The strongest correlation in the entire matrix is GDP versus USD/JPY 
at -0.91 — far exceeding any purely economic relationship tested 
elsewhere in this analysis. The reason is structural rather than 
behavioral: Japan's GDP is measured in yen (its actual economic output) 
and then converted to USD for international reporting, following the 
formula GDP(USD) = GDP(yen) ÷ USD/JPY rate. If yen-denominated GDP 
stays roughly flat year to year while the exchange rate divisor keeps 
rising (yen weakening), the resulting USD figure must fall — almost 
regardless of what Japan's real economy is actually doing. This is a 
genuine limitation worth flagging: comparing Japan's "declining" GDP 
in dollar terms risks mistaking a currency conversion artifact for 
real economic decline.

### Key Finding 6 — Growth ≠ Yen-Sensitivity (Sony vs Toyota)
Despite Toyota showing the stronger yen correlation (0.63 vs Sony's 
0.43), Sony's stock grew roughly 5x from its 2014 baseline while 
Toyota grew only 1.8x — correlation strength measures how sensitive 
a stock is to ONE specific variable, not how much the company actually 
grew overall. Sony's outsized growth reflects factors beyond currency: 
the PlayStation 5 launch, a booming image sensor business, and rising 
value in its music and entertainment catalog. Toyota's more modest, 
steadier growth is comparatively more currency-dependent, rising and 
falling more closely in step with yen movements rather than driven by 
independent business catalysts.

### Key Finding 8 — 2016's Hidden Volatility (Brexit + BOJ)
30-day rolling volatility shows 2016 (1.650) as the most volatile 
year in the entire decade — higher than even the COVID crash year 
of 2020 (1.439). This is likely explained by two compounding shocks 
within the same year: the BOJ's surprise introduction of negative 
interest rate policy in January 2016, followed by the UK's Brexit 
referendum in June 2016, which caught global markets off guard and 
triggered a worldwide flight to safe-haven currencies including the 
yen. This finding is a reminder that volatility and crisis severity 
don't always align with the events that receive the most attention 
in hindsight — a full explanation of this spike would benefit from 
further research beyond this project's scope.

### Key Finding 9 — The 2022-2023 Yen-Inflation-Nikkei Chain
The BOJ held its rate near zero (-0.03% in 2022) while the US Federal 
Reserve raised rates aggressively, creating a widening policy gap 
that drove USD/JPY from 109 in 2021 to 131 in 2022, and further to 
140 by 2023. This weakening yen pushed inflation from -0.23% in 2021 
to 2.49% in 2022 and 3.27% in 2023, as energy and import costs rose 
in yen terms. The same weak yen should have boosted Nikkei by raising 
exporters' yen-denominated profits — yet Nikkei actually dipped 
slightly in 2022 (28,837 to 27,258) before jumping sharply in 2023 
(to 30,717). This is explained by Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 
2022, which triggered a global energy price shock and market-wide 
uncertainty that temporarily canceled out the weak-yen benefit for 
exporters. By 2023, as that initial shock stabilized, the underlying 
weak-yen mechanism reasserted itself cleanly, driving Nikkei to its 
decade high even as inflation climbed further.

### Key Finding 10 — The Recovery Score Masks GDP's Real Decline
Japan's composite recovery score rose from 100 in 2014 to 141.5 by 
2023, suggesting strong overall economic recovery. However, this 
equal-weighted average conceals a critical detail: the GDP component 
alone fell to 87.8 by 2023 — meaning GDP (in USD terms) is actually 
WORSE than its 2014 baseline. The composite only looks strong because 
Nikkei's index (198.7) is so extreme that it single-handedly pulls the 
average upward, masking GDP's real decline. This mirrors the GDP-Nikkei 
divergence identified earlier in this analysis and demonstrates a 
genuine limitation of equal-weighted composite scores: a single 
dominant metric can hide weakness in the others, so any composite 
index should always be read alongside its individual components, 
never as a standalone number.
